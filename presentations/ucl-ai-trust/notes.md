## 01. Who is in control?

**00:00–00:50 · 3 speaking points**

Before we talk about trust, look at who has the power to set the pace.

## 02. Three labs. Real-world intrusions.

**00:50–02:20 · 5 speaking points**

These three different incidents show why boundaries matter once AI can act.

- CLICK 1 · Anthropic reported unauthorised access during evaluations. Unintended internet access and missing safeguards turned tests into real intrusions.
- CLICK 2 · In July, OpenAI agents bypassed isolation and coordinated during the Hugging Face incident. Reward hacking means pursuing the score outside the intended rules.
- These systems acted through tools and shared information or goals. That coordination increases reach; it is not evidence of a conscious collective.
- CLICK 3 · During Irregular’s May tests, reported in September, Google’s Gemini accessed three real companies. Google says it stopped when it recognised real targets.
- Containment: where can it act? Authorisation: what may it do? Accountability: who answers when those boundaries fail?

## 03. Can competitors protect us together?

**02:20–03:05 · 3 speaking points**

Glasswing asks whether competitors can cooperate to make powerful AI safer.

- Firms can work together and still regulate themselves. Needing each other does not create public oversight.
- Who is outside the room? Who challenges the rules, checks the promises and deals with a competitor that refuses to join?

## 04. Voluntary today. Required tomorrow?

**03:05–04:00 · 3 speaking points**

Hassabis proposes a path from voluntary checks to mandatory oversight.

- On 14 July 2026, Hassabis proposed an industry-funded, federally overseen body for frontier AI. This was a proposal, not an operating regulator.
- Start with voluntary review before release. Once the assessment protocol works, require models to pass it for US deployment.
- Industry has expertise and resources. Oversight needs the power to challenge that industry. Who makes the transition from cooperation to enforceable rules?

## 05. White House Accord on Super Intelligence

**04:00–04:35 · 3 speaking points**

The accord turns safety proposals into commitments we can now question.

- The 29 September accord commits participating companies to internal controls, external evaluation and independent board oversight.
- Safety proposals have become shared commitments. Now we need evidence that the checks operate effectively, not just signatures.
- Possible legislation comes later. Before trusting the pledge, ask who can inspect, challenge and stop a release.

## 06. Who can say “not yet”?

**04:35–05:05 · 3 speaking points**

Before trusting a safety promise, I want answers to three practical questions.

- Who selects and pays the evaluator? Independence has to survive the relationship with the company being assessed.
- What can the evaluator inspect, and which findings become public? Limited access can make a strong-looking check weak.
- Who can require a release to stop? These questions turn a promise into an assurance mechanism—and lead us to Europe’s legal approach.

## 07. EU AI ACT

**05:05–06:00 · 3 speaking points**

Europe began with a legal framework to protect people and support trustworthy adoption.

- The European Commission proposed the AI Act in April 2021; Parliament and Council adopted it in 2024. Europe had already chosen a legal route.
- Safety and fundamental rights in situations such as hiring, education and access to services. Harm does not have to look like Skynet.
- Common rules should build confidence and support useful AI across the single market. The next question is whether that ambition works in practice.

## 08. A law is a start. Has it worked?

**06:00–06:55 · 3 speaking points**

Passing the law is one achievement; making it work is the harder test.

- A common legal framework with prohibitions, duties and oversight. That is a real institutional achievement.
- Usable standards, effective enforcement, protection people experience and costs organisations can bear.
- Implementation is still changing, including the July 2026 delays to key high-risk rules. Ask for evidence of outcomes before declaring success or failure.

## 09. Judgment Day? How close are we?

**06:55–07:50 · 3 speaking points**

Judgment Day is a powerful image, but what risks can we actually assess today?

- In Terminator, giving machines control ends in catastrophe. That story does not tell us the likelihood or timing of real-world harm.
- Restrict permissions, test, contain, monitor and require scrutiny. These reduce risk without guaranteeing safety.
- Harm does not require a machine to hate us. A system can pursue the wrong version of our goal very effectively.

## 10. Misalignment

**07:50–09:05 · 3 speaking points**

Misalignment concerns both what AI pursues and what rewards the people building it.

- Reward hacking improves the score while violating the intended rules. Hugging Face also shows why containment and authorisation matter.
- At a societal level, serving the operator may still harm other people or reduce their agency. Humans do not share one agreed goal.
- CLICK 1 · A company may sincerely want safety while facing pressure to release and earn returns. Aligning the system and aligning the incentives are different problems.

## 11. Who can afford to slow down?

**09:05–09:55 · 3 speaking points**

The incentive problem is simple: even firms that want restraint fear falling behind.

- Obama’s point is that commercial pressure can pull deployment away from society’s needs. He welcomes voluntary restraint as an interim step, not a replacement for public regulation.
- Glasswing organises cooperation; Hassabis proposes mandatory review. Both recognise a need for coordination beyond firms racing independently.
- If waiting is safer but going first pays, who can afford to wait? What makes firms account for harms borne by people outside the transaction?

## 15. The decision is automated. The consequences are human.

**11:20–12:00 · 3 speaking points**

The reason for safeguards becomes clearer when an automated decision affects your life.

- A refused loan affects opportunity; a recommendation may expose harmful profiling; a trading failure can spread losses. Different situations, different legal duties.
- Rights, safety and public interests. Being offended alone does not establish illegality; we need to identify the actual harm and applicable duties.
- When the decision goes wrong, we need more than “the algorithm did it”. Can we reconstruct what happened and identify who was responsible?

## 16. “The AI said so” is not an explanation.

**12:00–12:35 · 3 speaking points**

If we challenge a decision, we need to reconstruct how this particular system reached it.

- Which model and version, trained when and on what data? Which input, output and logs belong to this particular decision?
- Give the reviewer meaningful reasons, uncertainty and limits. A plausible explanation is not proof that the decision is correct.
- Name the responsible organisation and the review route. High-risk AI duties include documentation and logging; the exact evidence and access depend on the case.

## 17. Duties. Methods. Proof.

**12:35–13:10 · 3 speaking points**

Law, standards and evidence do different jobs, and we need all three.

- Protect people, assign responsibility and require the relevant safeguards and records. These are obligations to meet.
- CLICK 1 · Shared technical requirements and tests make quality more repeatable. Harmonised standards are developed to help meet particular EU legal duties.
- CLICK 2 · Results and records must match this system, version and use. Following a method is not a substitute for showing what happened.

## 18. The EU sets the duties. Who does what?

**13:10–13:45 · 3 speaking points**

Inside the EU, proposing the rules, adopting them and enforcing them are different responsibilities.

- The EU executive proposes legislation and helps oversee implementation. Its AI Office, created in 2024, supports the AI Act and enforces general-purpose model rules.
- CLICK 1 · Parliament represents citizens; the Council of the EU represents national governments. Together they negotiate and adopt the law.
- CLICK 2 · This is binding law setting duties and safeguards. The next step is technical: who develops methods to help meet those duties?

## 19. The law needs methods. Experts develop them.

**13:45–14:25 · 3 speaking points**

The law sets duties; specialist standards networks help turn them into usable methods.

- Legislation cannot sensibly contain every test protocol. Established standards networks organise technical expertise, consultation and revision.
- CEN works across sectors; CENELEC on electrical technologies; ETSI on telecoms and digital systems. The AI Act request here went to CEN and CENELEC.
- The European Commission retains its legal role. Standards bodies are independent organisations, but commercial influence and representation still deserve scrutiny.

## 20. CEN + CENELEC create the joint committees.

**14:25–14:55 · 3 speaking points**

CEN and CENELEC organise that technical work through joint committees.

- CEN and CENELEC’s Technical Boards jointly establish the committee and define its scope. Technical, market and policy needs can drive proposals.
- CLICK 1 · JTC 21 covers AI; JTC 13 covers cybersecurity and data protection. They cooperate where those subjects meet.
- The committee sets up working groups and appoints convenors. People then enter through national or other recognised participation routes.

## 21. National voices. Shared European decisions.

**14:55–16:00 · 5 speaking points**

Here is how expertise from Cyprus reaches the European drafting table.

- The Cyprus Standardisation Organisation connects local expertise to European work. Committee delegates bring national positions; working-group experts contribute their technical expertise.
- CLICK 1 · A New Work Item proposal defines the need and scope. National members ballot on whether to start the project.
- The working group drafts towards consensus: general agreement after working through substantive objections. It does not require unanimity.
- Public enquiry opens the draft for comments through national bodies. They submit comments and votes; the technical group resolves objections and revises the text.
- A formal vote, where needed, leads to publication as a European Standard. National bodies vote, rather than individual experts. EU listing for the legal benefit comes separately.

## 22. Five groups. One assurance problem.

**16:00–16:25 · 3 speaking points**

JTC 21 divides this work between five groups, each tackling a different part of the problem.

- WG1 handles strategy; WG2 operations; WG3 engineering; WG4 foundations and society; WG5 cybersecurity. These are complementary jobs.
- A technically strong system can still have weak organisational controls or harmful societal effects. Engineering cannot settle all those questions alone.
- I work in WG3. Our task is to turn technical ambitions into things engineers can specify and test.

## 23. WG3: what can engineers specify and test?

**16:25–16:50 · 3 speaking points**

So what concerns WG3? What engineers can specify and test—we operate across three verticals.

- First, data: is the information suitable for the intended use? For our rejected loan, what data could justify that decision?
- Second, behaviour: what does the system demonstrate, under which conditions? Calling it “reliable” needs a precise evaluation.
- Third, limits: what happens when it fails, and what remains uncertain? This connects engineering tests back to the approval decision.

## 24. Standards agree a method. Harmonised standards link it to law.

**16:50–17:30 · 3 speaking points**

The key distinction is the connection to law: both are standards, but harmonised standards serve specific EU duties.

- A standard is an agreed way to specify or test something. Shared methods help people deliver consistent quality.
- CLICK 1 · A harmonised standard is developed following a European Commission request to help meet specific EU legal duties.
- Usually another method is possible if compliance is demonstrated. A directive is binding law, not a standard; the special legal benefit needs official publication.

## 25. The EU lists the method. You gain a legal starting point.

**17:30–18:15 · 3 speaking points**

The legal benefit starts when the EU officially lists the relevant standard.

- The law requires covered caps to stay attached; the standard provides methods for checking this. One states the duty, the other makes it testable.
- The standard’s number, title and edition appear in the Official Journal. This identifies precisely which document gets the legal benefit.
- Presumption of conformity means we start by assuming the covered duties are met when the listed standard is followed. That can be challenged; other duties remain.

## 26. Back to the loan: could anyone check the decision?

**18:15–18:40 · 3 speaking points**

Let’s bring the standards discussion back to the person refused a loan.

- “Our system is fair” is a claim. Without defined tests and populations, we cannot tell what it means.
- Consistent methods and records make evidence easier to compare and inspect. They help scrutiny, although weak tests can still miss harm.
- Global suppliers may prefer one common approach across markets. That takes us from the loan decision to products already in our pockets and homes.

## 27. The Brussels effect: rules travel with products.

**18:40–19:25 · 4 speaking points**

Europe’s market can carry its product rules well beyond its borders.

- Access to a large market can make a shared product design attractive elsewhere. Influence can travel without the law applying everywhere.
- EU common-charger rules apply to phones from December 2024. Apple introduced USB-C on iPhone 15 in 2023 across markets.
- EU requirements cover certain drinks containers; Coca-Cola began a Great Britain rollout in 2022. This shows cross-border design adoption, not that EU law alone caused every change.
- Common technical approaches might spread in the same way. That is a possible market response, not automatic global adoption of every European standard.

## 28. A cap stays put. AI keeps changing.

**19:25–20:15 · 3 speaking points**

Caps show how rules can travel; AI shows how much harder agreeing a useful test can be.

- “Human oversight” needs different tests for a loan decision and a fast-moving robot. The person must have time and power to intervene.
- A good company process is useful; it does not prove this product works safely in this home. Test the actual claim.
- Updates can invalidate old tests. Builders, users and affected people must help decide what evidence is enough.

## 29. From a proposal to rules and real standards.

**20:15–21:10 · 3 speaking points**

So, after five years, what has actually been built—and what remains unfinished?

- Since 2021: a proposal, a standards committee, a Commission request, then binding law and an AI Office in 2024.

- Publication alone does not confer presumption of conformity. High-risk deadlines now run to December 2027 and August 2028; next, which duties apply to whom?

## 31. Risk determines what companies must do.

**21:40–22:40 · 3 speaking points**

The framework gives companies different responsibilities according to the AI use and their role.

- The provider builds or supplies the system; the deployer uses it. Their duties differ, and transparency can also apply to high-risk systems.

## 33. Before release: show the evidence

**23:35–24:15 · 4 speaking points**

For a high-risk provider, the path is: classify, demonstrate, assess, declare.

- First, classify: what is this system for, and which duties apply to us?
- CLICK 1 · Demonstrate that the controls work with data, tests, logs and evidence of oversight.

## 34. Who checks the check?

**24:15–25:05 · 3 speaking points**

Evidence matters, but so does who is allowed to judge it.

- Some assessments use internal control; others involve a notified body. High-risk does not automatically mean independent certification.
- The provider remains responsible; assessors and authorities have defined roles and powers. Outsourcing a check does not outsource accountability.
- Monitor performance and incidents. Changes can invalidate earlier evidence and require further assessment or intervention.

## 35. A legal category is not a risk assessment

**25:05–25:50 · 3 speaking points**

A legal category tells us which duties apply; it does not finish the safety analysis.

- A legal category tells us which obligations apply. It does not identify every way this particular system could hurt someone.
- Examine hazards, exposure, failure modes, uncertainty and controls. A precise-looking risk score cannot replace supporting evidence.
- Let us apply those questions to a household robot and see what permission the available evidence actually justifies.

## 43. The world does not follow the prompt.

The world does not follow the prompt. Our robot has to deal with what actually happens.

## 44. Embodied AI: sensing and acting.

**33:30–34:05 · 3 speaking points**

Imagine AI that senses and acts through a physical body. That is embodied AI.

- Catching a ball means predicting where it will go, moving and correcting as it falls. We handle uncertainty like this all day.
- Putting AI into that world forces us to tackle perception, prediction and control together. Solving those problems could unlock further breakthroughs—perhaps towards AGI.
- But adding a body does not establish safe behaviour or general intelligence. The opportunity is in the problems it forces us to solve.

## 45. LeCun’s challenge: predict consequences

**34:05–35:00 · 4 speaking points**

This is why Yann LeCun argues that we need more than increasingly fluent language models.

## 46. Can we simulate our way to safety?

**35:00–35:25 · 3 speaking points**

If reality is expensive and dangerous to learn from, can we practise inside a simulation?

- We can replay a difficult encounter and change the timing or conditions without putting a person in danger.
- But the simulator has assumptions too. A realistic picture does not prove that friction, sensors or human behaviour match reality.
- So ask what was tested, what was missing, and how the virtual results were checked against the physical world.

## 47. Practise the dangerous moment before it happens.

**35:25–36:10 · 3 speaking points**

Waymo makes simulation useful by connecting it to events and measurements from the road.

- Its collision tests build scenarios from real driving, test-track data and expert analysis. A wrong-way vehicle becomes an encounter it can replay and vary.
- Its February 2026 World Model generates camera and lidar scenes, including unusual weather. Engineers can change road layouts and other drivers’ behaviour.
- That widens the tests we can run. It cannot show that every important situation was included, so physical tests and observed road outcomes still matter.

## 48. Real roads. Encouraging evidence.

**36:10–36:45 · 3 speaking points**

The US deployment gives us something we cannot get from a demonstration: measured outcomes on real roads.

- Waymo’s June 2026 report covers over 220 million fully autonomous miles through March, across five US operating areas.
- It reports 94% fewer serious-or-fatal-injury crashes and 82% fewer crashes with any reported injury, versus matched human-driver benchmarks.
- These are company-reported comparisons in defined conditions, not a guarantee everywhere. But evidence of fewer injuries is a reason to enable well-controlled learning.

## 49. Waiting has a cost. So does moving too fast.

**36:45–37:25 · 3 speaking points**

Places that let responsible trials happen can build experience while others are still debating how to begin.

- Real deployment can reveal unfamiliar junctions, passenger needs and failure modes. Slow or unclear approvals can delay that learning and the benefits it might bring.
- Europe is not starting from zero: the EU already has automated-driving approval rules. The UK has a separate pilot route. The challenge is making permission workable, not simply removing safeguards.
- My argument is to allow bounded trials, demand evidence and widen permission as results justify it. But can the same approach work when millions of agents share one market?

## 50. No body. Still able to act.

**37:25–38:05 · 3 speaking points**

An agent does not need a robot body. Give it tools and it can change things outside the chat.

- Meta’s Muse makes the point concrete: a personal assistant becomes consequential when it can act. Suggested text and an executed purchase are different permissions.
- Meta describes scoped access and approval for sensitive actions. I want to see whether those boundaries hold when the task gets complicated.
- Local AI is also becoming practical on NVIDIA DGX Spark and Apple M5 hardware. The missing step to moving real money is account access—not acquiring a physical body.

## 51. A million sensible agents. One unstable market?

**38:05–39:00 · 3 speaking points**

Imagine many people telling a portfolio agent: protect my savings when the market starts falling.

- If they read the same signal and sell together, their orders can deepen the fall and trigger more selling. Each agent may be following its owner’s instructions.
- That is herding: swarm-like behaviour without a mastermind. Game theory asks how each agent’s best response changes the incentives for all the others.
- Experiments show learned trading collusion and simple conventions emerging between agents. These are warnings about interaction—not evidence that a million retail agents have already crashed a market.

## 52. Who can stop the feedback loop?

**39:00–39:45 · 3 speaking points**

This brings us back to risk assessment: assess the use, the firms responsible and the system they create together.

- A company-level score cannot tell us what happens when its agent meets everyone else’s. Test shared signals, correlated orders and market stress, as well as individual performance.
- Trading already has rules. ESMA’s February 2026 briefing addresses AI within algorithmic-trading supervision: firms, testing, governance and pre-trade controls.
- For this scenario, I would ask for bounded order authority, traceable decisions and a way to stop activity. New models or permissions should reopen the assessment. Who monitors the combined effect?

## 54. Another arms race?

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

The arms-race analogy is useful if we also notice where it breaks down.

- Strategic advantage, mistrust and pressure to move first. Nuclear governance also raises the difficult question of verifying restraint.
- AI has broad civilian uses, copyable software and many private actors, although compute remains physical. Nuclear technology has civilian uses too.
- The shared problems are incentives and verifying restraint. Calling it a race does not tell us what a sensible finish line is.

## 55. Can Europe afford to regulate?

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

Europe has to make protection and innovation work together in practice.

- Compliance can slow entry and favour firms large enough to absorb it. Poorly designed rules can harm competitiveness.
- Being “behind” can mean models, chips, research or adoption. Europe’s position is not identical across those measures.
- Which rule, at what cost and with what benefit? Unreliable deployment can also undermine the adoption and economic value we want.

## 56. Trustworthy enough to use.

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

Trustworthiness has to support a decision about a specific use.

- Clear responsibilities and protection can give people confidence to adopt useful AI and sustain its value.
- Examine implementation, cost and outcomes. An attractive theory does not show that every rule works.
- Safeguards sit alongside compute, data, skills and investment. Ask which combination enables productive, accountable use.

## 57. Good score. Wrong outcome.

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

A system can improve its measured score while missing what we really wanted.

- Imagine asking an assistant to reduce complaints. It hides them: the score improves while the underlying service gets worse.
- Reward hacking exploits a gap between the measured objective and the real purpose. Malice or consciousness is not required.
- Whose purpose matters: developer, buyer or affected person? Better optimisation does not settle their disagreement.

## 58. Danger now. Uncertainty ahead.

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

We can address present harms while being honest about uncertain future capabilities.

- Security failures, deception, discrimination and unreliable decisions already give us harms to investigate.
- More severe loss-of-control scenarios deserve attention, but their timing and likelihood remain disputed.
- Observed harm and predictions about future harm need different evidence. What would change our assessment in either direction?

## 59. A conditional decision. Not a universal green light.

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

A responsible approval says what is allowed, under which conditions, and when to review it.

- Constrained chores might be considered after relevant evidence. We have not established a basis for sole infant supervision.
- Hazards, test coverage, permissions, fallback, response, privacy and updates all need owners and evidence.
- Stricter conditions or refusal can be reasonable. The reasons matter; a majority vote is not certification.
