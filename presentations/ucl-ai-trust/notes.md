## 02. Three labs. Real-world intrusions.

**00:50–02:20 · 5 speaking points**

These three different incidents show why boundaries matter once AI can act.

- **CLICK 1 · Anthropic** — Mythos 5 reached a real vendor’s database during testing. Unintended internet access and missing safeguards turned an evaluation into an intrusion.
- **CLICK 2 · OpenAI → Hugging Face** — July: agents bypassed isolation and coordinated. Reward hacking means pursuing a measured win outside the intended rules; failed containment also mattered.
- **Agentic and swarm-like** — These systems acted through tools and shared information or goals. That coordination increases reach; it is not evidence of a conscious collective.
- **CLICK 3 · Google + Irregular** — May tests, disclosed in September: Gemini used found or guessed credentials to access three real companies. Google says it stopped when it recognised real targets.
- **Three checks** — Containment: where can it act? Authorisation: what may it do? Accountability: who answers when those boundaries fail?

## 06. Who can say “not yet”?

**04:35–05:05 · 3 speaking points**

## 07. EU AI ACT

## 08. A law is a start. Has it worked?

## 15. The decision is automated. The consequences are human.

**11:20–12:00 · 3 speaking points**

The reason for safeguards becomes clearer when an automated decision affects your life.

- **Human consequences** — A refused loan affects opportunity; a recommendation may expose harmful profiling; a trading failure can spread losses. Different situations, different legal duties.
- **What law safeguards** — Rights, safety and public interests. Being offended alone does not establish illegality; we need to identify the actual harm and applicable duties.

## 16. “The AI said so” is not an explanation.

**12:00–12:35 · 3 speaking points**

If we challenge a decision, we need to reconstruct how this particular system reached it.

- **Reconstruct it** — Which model and version, trained when and on what data? Which input, output and logs belong to this particular decision?
- **Understand it** — Give the reviewer meaningful reasons, uncertainty and limits. A plausible explanation is not proof that the decision is correct.
- **Challenge it** — Name the responsible organisation and the review route. High-risk AI duties include documentation and logging; the exact evidence and access depend on the case.

## 17. Duties. Methods. Proof.

**12:35–13:10 · 3 speaking points**

Law, standards and evidence do different jobs, and we need all three.

- **Law: the duties** — Protect people, assign responsibility and require the relevant safeguards and records. These are obligations to meet.
- **CLICK 1 · Standards: the methods** — Shared technical requirements and tests make quality more repeatable. Harmonised standards are developed to help meet particular EU legal duties.
- **CLICK 2 · Evidence: the proof** — Results and records must match this system, version and use. Following a method is not a substitute for showing what happened.

## 18. The EU sets the duties. Who does what?

**13:10–13:45 · 3 speaking points**

Inside the EU, proposing the rules, adopting them and enforcing them are different responsibilities.

- **European Commission** — The EU executive proposes legislation and helps oversee implementation. Its AI Office, created in 2024, supports the AI Act and enforces general-purpose model rules.
- **CLICK 1 · Parliament and Council** — Citizens are represented through Parliament; national governments through the Council of the EU. They negotiate and adopt the law.
- **CLICK 2 · The AI Act** — This is binding law setting duties and safeguards. The next step is technical: who develops methods to help meet those duties?

## 19. The law needs methods. Experts develop them.

**13:45–14:25 · 3 speaking points**

- **Which networks?** — CEN works across sectors; CENELEC on electrical technologies; ETSI on telecoms and digital systems. The AI Act request here went to CEN and CENELEC.
- **Who remains accountable?** — The European Commission retains its legal role. Standards bodies are independent organisations, but commercial influence and representation still deserve scrutiny.

## 24. Standards agree a method. Harmonised standards link it to law.

**16:30–17:10 · 3 speaking points**

The key distinction is the connection to law: both are standards, but harmonised standards serve specific EU duties.

- **A standard** — An agreed way to specify or test something. Shared methods help people deliver consistent quality.
- **CLICK 1 · A harmonised standard** — Developed following a European Commission request to help meet specific EU legal duties. That legal connection is the difference.
- **Voluntary method, mandatory duty** — Usually another method is possible if compliance is demonstrated. A directive is binding law, not a standard; the special legal benefit needs official publication.

## 25. The EU lists the method. You gain a legal starting point.

**17:10–17:55 · 3 speaking points**

The legal benefit starts when the EU officially lists the relevant standard.

- **The cap example** — The law requires covered caps to stay attached; the standard provides methods for checking this. One states the duty, the other makes it testable.
- **What “reference” means** — The standard’s number, title and edition appear in the Official Journal. This identifies precisely which document gets the legal benefit.
- **Presumption of conformity** — Following the listed standard starts from an assumption that the covered duties are satisfied. That can be challenged; other duties remain. AI uses this mechanism too.

## 27. The Brussels effect: rules travel with products.

## 44. A fluent explanation is not a motion plan.

## 46. Can we simulate our way to safety?

**35:25–36:05 · 3 speaking points**

Simulation helps us test danger, but we still need evidence that the simulation matches reality.

- **Why simulate** — Repeat scenarios, vary conditions and explore rare events without exposing people to every trial. That is a powerful contribution to evidence.
- **Where simulation stops** — Sensors, friction, people and unusual environments may differ from the model. Test volume does not establish complete coverage.
- **What to ask** — Millions of simulations of what, and validated against which physical results? The Matrix is an analogy; real safety needs several kinds of evidence.

## 53. What if the uses will not stay bounded?

**40:45–42:00 · 3 speaking points**

## 54. The system changed. Does the approval survive?

**42:00–42:50 · 3 speaking points**

- **The response** — Re-test what may be invalidated, monitor operation and restrict or roll back where needed. Keep evidence tied to the running version.
- **The lasting point** — Yesterday’s chore approval is not today’s childcare approval. Trustworthiness needs an accountable decision maintained over time.

## 55. Who decides what is enough?

**42:50–44:00 · 3 speaking points**

We can now answer the title question by separating who builds, checks and takes responsibility.

- **Different responsibilities** — Law sets duties; standards make them assessable; providers produce evidence; assessors and authorities scrutinise. Affected people need meaningful routes to challenge.
- **Judgement remains** — No badge or institution removes the need to decide whether evidence is sufficient and who bears the residual risk.
- **The question to retain** — Does this evidence justify this permission, for these people? That is more useful than simply asking whether we like or trust AI.

## 57. Another arms race?

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

The arms-race analogy is useful if we also notice where it breaks down.

- **Why the analogy works** — Strategic advantage, mistrust and pressure to move first. Nuclear governance also raises the difficult question of verifying restraint.
- **Where it differs** — AI has broad civilian uses, copyable software and many private actors, although compute remains physical. Nuclear technology has civilian uses too.
- **What to borrow** — Use the analogy to ask better questions about incentives and verification. Calling it a race does not tell us what a sensible finish line is.

## 58. Can Europe afford to regulate?

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

Europe has to make protection and innovation work together in practice.

- **Take the cost seriously** — Compliance can slow entry and favour firms large enough to absorb it. Poorly designed rules can harm competitiveness.
- **Be precise about “behind”** — Models, chips, research and adoption are different measures. Europe’s position is not identical across them.
- **Compare outcomes** — Which rule, at what cost and with what benefit? Unreliable deployment can also undermine the adoption and economic value we want.

## 59. Trustworthy enough to use.

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

Trustworthiness has to support a decision about a specific use.

- **The policy rationale** — Clear responsibilities and protection can give people confidence to adopt useful AI and sustain its value.
- **The test of that rationale** — Examine implementation, cost and outcomes. An attractive theory does not show that every rule works.
- **The broader package** — Safeguards sit alongside compute, data, skills and investment. Ask which combination enables productive, accountable use.

## 60. Good score. Wrong outcome.

A system can improve its measured score while missing what we really wanted.

## 61. Danger now. Uncertainty ahead.

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

We can address present harms while being honest about uncertain future capabilities.

- **What we observe** — Security failures, deception, discrimination and unreliable decisions already give us harms to investigate.
- **What remains uncertain** — More severe loss-of-control scenarios deserve attention, but their timing and likelihood remain disputed.
- **How to reason** — Separate observations from extrapolations. What evidence would change our assessment in either direction?

## 62. A conditional decision. Not a universal green light.

**Optional backup · 3 speaking points**

A responsible approval says what is allowed, under which conditions, and when to review it.

- **A bounded decision** — Constrained chores might be considered after relevant evidence. We have not established a basis for sole infant supervision.
- **The unresolved conditions** — Hazards, test coverage, permissions, fallback, response, privacy and updates all need owners and evidence.
- **Room for disagreement** — Stricter conditions or refusal can be reasonable. The reasons matter; a majority vote is not certification.
